# Research, Testing, and Measurement

Use this reference when the request involves personas, journey maps, content hypotheses, usability studies, A/B testing, analytics, or measuring UX writing outcomes.

## Contents

- Evidence discipline
- Research framing
- Content hypotheses
- Evaluation methods
- Metrics
- Reporting limitations

## Evidence Discipline

Separate:

- **Observed**: directly present in artifacts, behavior, research, or data;
- **Reported**: stated by a participant or stakeholder;
- **Inferred**: a reasonable interpretation requiring validation;
- **Proposed**: a design or wording recommendation;
- **Verified**: supported by an appropriate completed check.

Do not create fictional personas, quotes, pain points, sample sizes, metrics, or research results.

## Research Framing

Before recommending research, define:

- decision the team needs to make;
- audience and task;
- content or pattern being compared;
- risk of being wrong;
- evidence already available;
- smallest method that can reduce the uncertainty.

Use existing user research, support evidence, search logs, product feedback, and analytics when available and authorized. Treat stakeholder opinion as input, not user evidence.

## Content Hypotheses

Use:

> For [audience] trying to [task] in [context], changing [content variable] from [current] to [proposed] should improve [observable outcome] because [reason]. We will consider the hypothesis supported if [criterion].

Define one primary uncertainty per test. Keep the behavior, visual design, targeting, and timing stable where possible when testing wording.

## Evaluation Methods

### Heuristic Review

Useful for identifying likely problems in clarity, consistency, recovery, trust, inclusion, and fit. It does not prove user performance.

### Comprehension or Cloze Test

Useful for whether people understand terminology or can predict missing language. It does not prove task completion in the product.

### Usability Test

Useful for observing comprehension, decisions, task success, hesitation, and recovery in context. Use realistic tasks and avoid leading participants toward the wording under test.

### Preference Test

Useful only for subjective tone or preference questions. Do not treat preference as evidence of comprehension or performance.

### A/B Test

Useful when traffic, instrumentation, ethical conditions, and a meaningful behavioral metric exist. Define exposure, primary metric, guardrails, segment, duration, and stopping rule before interpreting results.

### Accessibility and Localization Review

Use qualified reviewers and actual assistive technologies or target-language expertise for claims beyond static design inspection.

## Metrics

Match metrics to the content’s job:

- orientation: correct destination choice, reduced backtracking;
- form guidance: completion, validation error, correction, abandonment;
- error recovery: successful recovery, repeat failure, support contact;
- onboarding: first useful action, completion, skip, return;
- destructive confirmation: cancellation of unintended action, support incidents;
- help content: task completion, escalation, repeated search;
- notification or status: correct understanding of system state;
- terminology: comprehension, search success, support terminology alignment.

Conversion alone is not a universal UX-writing success metric. Include trust, error, support, and downstream quality guardrails when persuasion could conflict with user interests.

## Reporting Limitations

State:

- method and tested artifact;
- participant or data source when available;
- sample and segment limitations;
- what changed besides content;
- observed result versus interpretation;
- uncertainty and alternative explanations;
- whether findings generalize beyond the tested context.

Do not describe a recommended test as completed, a directional signal as statistically conclusive, or an unrepresentative sample as universal evidence.
