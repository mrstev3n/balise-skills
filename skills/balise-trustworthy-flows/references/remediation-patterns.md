# Cooperative remediation patterns

Use the smallest change that repairs the mechanism while preserving the legitimate product objective.

## Disclosure before commitment

- Show total price, mandatory fees, recurrence, first charge, renewal, duration, data use, recipients, and consequential limits before the final action.
- Keep essential information visible. A detail link may supplement but not replace decision-critical facts.
- Repeat material facts at the commitment point when earlier context may no longer be salient.

## Choice symmetry

- Use direct labels for accept, refuse, defer, customize, cancel, and delete.
- Compare branches by steps, visibility, search, reading, authentication, channel, and delay.
- Keep opposing options discoverable in the same decision context when appropriate.
- Do not require visual identity at the pixel level. Match salience and effort to consequence rather than commercial preference.

## Defaults and active choice

- Require active choice for significant cost, recurring commitment, data processing, permission, or waiver.
- Use safe defaults when inaction could cause harm.
- Explain a protective default without framing the alternative as shameful.

## Refusal and repeated requests

- Persist refusal.
- Ask again only after a meaningful, explainable change of context.
- State why the request returned and retain a durable decline path.
- Do not replace refusal with an indefinite “later” when a durable refusal is possible.

## Cancellation, withdrawal, and exit

- Provide cancellation in the expected account or product context.
- Avoid repeated retention offers, artificial authentication, channel switching, or unnecessary delay.
- State the effective date, retained access, future charge status, data consequences, and restoration options.
- Confirm the result and keep a record accessible to the person when appropriate.

## Destructive and consequential actions

Prefer this order:

1. prevent the error;
2. make the action reversible;
3. support review and correction;
4. use a precise confirmation when the action remains rare and irreversible.

Use explicit verbs and identify the object and consequence. Prefer undo, trash, grace periods, and recovery where technically and product-appropriate. Repetitive generic confirmation creates habituation.

WCAG 2.2 criterion 3.3.4 specifically requires at least one of reversibility, input checking with correction, or review and confirmation for pages causing legal commitments or financial transactions, modifying or deleting user-controllable stored data, or submitting test responses. Applying the principle elsewhere is a design extension, not a claim about WCAG scope.

## Permissions and consent

- Ask at the moment the feature needs the capability.
- Explain what is needed, why, whether it is optional, and what limited experience remains after refusal.
- Do not imitate a system permission prompt or bias its controls.
- Keep current status and a stable revocation path visible.
- Distinguish service necessity from a benefit to the product.

For cookies and similar trackers in the French CNIL context, preserve the same degree of simplicity for accept and refuse and make withdrawal as simple as giving consent. Similar same-screen salience is strongly recommended; strict pixel identity is not a universal rule. Recheck the current recommendation and FAQ before making a legal claim.

## Pricing, scarcity, urgency, and social proof

- Normalize units, periods, inclusions, and total payable for comparison.
- Show real-currency equivalents for credits or tokens.
- Display scarcity or urgency only when the system has accurate, scoped, dated evidence.
- State what happens after a genuine deadline.
- Identify the source, period, method, sponsorship, and relevance of social proof.
- Remove the signal when provenance cannot be established.

## Protective friction test

Keep asymmetric friction only when every condition holds:

- it protects the person from a concrete risk;
- it is necessary and the least restrictive viable measure;
- it is not justified only by conversion or retention;
- it applies consistently to decisions with comparable risk;
- it preserves a clear exit;
- its rationale and runtime dependency are documented.

## Accessibility safeguards

When applicable, account for WCAG 2.2:

- 3.3.1 Error Identification;
- 3.3.2 Labels or Instructions;
- 3.3.3 Error Suggestion;
- 3.3.4 Error Prevention (Legal, Financial, Data);
- 3.3.7 Redundant Entry;
- 2.5.2 Pointer Cancellation;
- 2.5.8 Target Size (Minimum);
- 3.2.6 Consistent Help;
- 4.1.3 Status Messages.

These criteria have distinct scopes and exceptions. A design artifact can show intent and measurements; runtime must prove semantics, focus, keyboard behavior, announcements, reflow, and assistive-technology support.

## Safety routing

Flag, preserve, and route rather than improvising when the flow may expose:

- a person seeking help on a monitored device or account;
- location, relationship, health, identity, or activity to a coercive actor;
- a survivor through notification, history, logs, or an obvious emergency exit;
- a child or other high-risk population to consequential pressure.

Do not invent a “quick escape” control, hidden history behavior, or safety workflow from general guidance. Require documented threat research and specialist review before changing the flow.
